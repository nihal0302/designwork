Work/Recruit CRM — Kanban board

A hiring board that could show you everything except what needed you.

Recruit CRM's Kanban board tracks candidates across eleven stages. Every stage showed the same card — and none of them showed how long anyone had been waiting. I rebuilt the card as a system that changes with the stage, and made time visible.

Problem

Where the board stopped helping

Context

Recruit CRM serves recruitment agencies. A recruiter there is not hiring for herself — she sits between a candidate and a client's hiring manager, and her day is spent working out who needs me right now across several open roles at once.

The Kanban board is where that judgement happens. But every column rendered the same card, whether the recruiter was screening three hundred applicants or chasing a signed offer. I spent time inside the live product with realistic data and logged 52 specific observations. They clustered into three problems.

01

Fixed information

Every stage showed identical fields. The resume — the artefact a screening decision rests on — wasn't on the card at all, while a job title identical across the whole board took a prime line.

02

No sense of time

Only an absolute date. A candidate untouched for a month looked exactly like one added this morning, so pipelines went stale silently and nobody noticed until someone was lost.

03

Poor at scale

Moving candidates forward meant a modal and a submit click each. At three hundred applicants the board became a wall of near-identical cards with no way to find what needed attention.

Design process

How I worked

Step 01
Learn the job before judging the tool

Agency recruiting has its own shape: a client who never logs in, decisions that arrive by email, and a recruiter accountable for a pace she doesn't control. I mapped the hiring process end to end before opening the product, so I'd recognise a real problem rather than an unfamiliar one.

Step 02
Fifty-two observations, pinned

I loaded realistic candidate data and moved people through all eleven stages, pinning every friction point directly onto screenshots. Working from evidence rather than memory meant each later design decision could be traced back to something I'd actually hit.

Step 03
One direct competitor, one lateral

Joboro, a direct rival, and Trello — the tool Recruit CRM's own documentation calls its board a version of. The lateral comparison turned out to matter more than the direct one.

Step 04
Extract, then design

I pulled the type scale, colour, spacing and elevation from the running application rather than eyeballing screenshots — then designed inside those constraints, so the work reads as the same product rather than a redesign bolted on.

The most useful constraint came out of that extraction: the product uses three type sizes — 12, 13 and 15px — and a 258px card. Every improvement had to fit that, which ruled out the easy answer of simply adding more to the card.
Field research

What the field showed

Finding

Joboro solves interaction well — actions live on the card, multi-select is a proper mode, and card density can be set per column. Those are answers worth borrowing for problems one and three.

But neither product surfaces ageing at all. Joboro shows no date anywhere. Recruit CRM stores exact stage-history timestamps and displays none of them. Meanwhile Trello — a general-purpose tool with no knowledge of recruiting — ships Card Aging to a quarter of a million boards, fading cards by how long they've been inactive.

The pattern the whole category is missing has existed for a decade in the tool it copied the board from.

Key decisions

Two calls worth defending

Decision 01

Ageing as a treatment, not an element

Anything added to show staleness — a badge, a chip, an extra row — makes cards taller, which makes the high-volume problem worse. The signal had to cost no vertical space.

✕
Rejected — fade the whole card

Trello's mechanic, but its meaning inverts here. A faded Trello card is ignorable; a stale candidate is urgent. Fading de-emphasises exactly what needs attention, and hurts legibility on the cards you most need to read.

Step 01
A spine on the left edge

A column is scanned vertically, so the eye already travels down the left margin. A three-pixel spine turns the column into a readable strip — two stale cards among forty are visible without reading a word, and the card stays fully legible.

Step 02
Thresholds weighted by stage

A stale card in Applied costs nothing; a stale card in Offered costs the placement. Amber and red arrive at thirty days in Applied and within days at the closing stages — the same visual language on different clocks.

Decision 02
One card, changing by stage

Up to the interview, the card is assessing a person. From Selected onward it is tracking a transaction. The same fields cannot serve both, so the card splits into two layers: a fixed action layer — contact, resume, notes — that never changes, and a context layer carrying only what the current stage needs.

The obvious alternative was to let recruiters pick their own fields. Zoho Recruit does exactly that, and it produces cards where the employer's name becomes the headline and a bare 6 floats with no label. Configurability without hierarchy makes cards worse, not better — so the density options are curated, not free-form.

Reflection

With more time

  • →
    Filters, at board and column level. The cheapest candidate to screen is the one never seen. Because each column now surfaces different fields, filtering needs to work per column as well as globally.
  • →
    A resume-first review loop. Multi-select solves filing — selecting many candidates and moving them together. It doesn't solve deciding: the recruiter still opens a resume, returns to the board, and opens the next.
  • →
    Testing the stage-specific cards with recruiters. The central bet is that a card changing between columns aids recognition rather than breaking it. That's genuinely unproven.
  • →
    Keyboard shortcuts. The user is a power user across several tools. A screening pass should be a rhythm, not a drag.
